International Online Symposium · June 1, 2026

Human–AI
Creative Workflows

The growing complexity arising from the integration of Generative AI into creative practices requires equally complex theoretical, conceptual, and methodological approaches across disciplines. This symposium brings together scholars, professionals and creative practitioners who study Human–AI creative workflows.

Spanning from different fields and areas, the symposium will present research papers examining how AI reshapes authorship, agency, labour, and creative processes. The symposium takes place fully online on June 1, 2026 and is free to attend.

Organized by scholars from Pompeu Fabra University (Barcelona), University College London, and the University of Amsterdam.

A
11:30–12:50 CET  ·  Chair: Szilvia Ruszev (UCL)
Creativity, Art and New Workflows
This paper argues that current debates on Human–AI creativity overlook a crucial temporal and operational dimension: the question of when, under what conditions, and through which sequences of mediation an output becomes legible as art. Drawing on Rancière, Steyerl, Wark, and Parikka, it proposes rethinking artistic legitimacy as an effect of workflows rather than an intrinsic property of objects or intentions.
This paper examines unintentional creative discovery and distributed authorship in film editing through practice-based master's research at the Film University Babelsberg, using artificial intelligence to stage serendipitous outcomes in the cutting room. It models a chained Human–AI workflow—cuts, interpretation into a prompt, and a second generative pass—with minimal steering, reflecting on productive chance and how co-creation with AI renegotiates editorial control.
This paper presents an ongoing practice-based experiment in generating short AI videos entirely on a local machine through chained models for image description, story generation, text-to-video, narration, and music. Developed through vibe coding, the workflow approaches AI slop from within, using its low-resolution and uncanny outputs to examine dataset afterlives, automation, agency, and authorship. It situates contemporary GenAI pipelines within longer histories of rule-based and delegated artistic production.
This paper examines how the logic of generative AI tools — their affordances, default outputs, and embedded aesthetic preferences — shapes and constrains the artistic freedom of creators who use them. Focusing on educational contexts, it investigates how instructors and learners negotiate the tension between AI's workflow logic and their own artistic intentions, developing a conceptual framework for understanding freedom within constrained generative systems.
B
13:30–14:50 CET  ·  Chair: Claudio Celis Bueno (UvA)
Industry, Labour and Distributed Agencies
Based on ethnographic fieldwork in a local television newsroom, this paper examines how journalists negotiate the integration of AI tools into professional workflows. The analysis focuses on the epistemic labour involved in keeping humans "in the loop" — the cognitive, procedural, and social work of verifying, contextualising, and overriding AI outputs — and how this labour reconfigures professional norms, authority, and newsroom culture.
Drawing on Stiegler's concept of grammatisation, this paper analyses how generative AI tools are transforming web design workflows by formalising, standardising, and automating previously tacit design knowledge. Through interviews with professional web designers and analysis of AI-assisted design platforms, it maps how grammatisation redistributes creative agency, reshapes professional expertise, and raises new questions about aesthetic judgment in Human–AI collaborative workflows.
This paper presents ethnographic research conducted within Italian advertising agencies adopting generative AI into their creative workflows. Focusing on the practices of selection and evaluation — the choices made between AI-generated and human-authored outputs — the study develops a framework of distributed agency to account for how creative decisions are co-produced across human and non-human actors.
Drawing on Foucault's author function and Actor-Network Theory, this paper examines how human writers negotiate authorship and creative control when co-writing with large language models. It maps the shifting distributions of agency in Human–AI co-authorship, exploring how writers assert, relinquish, and reclaim control across different stages of the writing process.
C
15:00–16:20 CET  ·  Chair: Bertran Salvador-Mata (UPF)
Authorship, Resistance and Learning
This paper investigates how the integration of generative AI into filmmaking education and professional practice is reshaping pedagogical frameworks, creative curricula, and the skill sets expected of emerging media professionals. Drawing on empirical research with educators and students, it addresses questions of AI literacy, the renegotiation of authorship in student work, and the broader ethical implications of AI-assisted filmmaking within educational institutions.
Bringing together Simondon's philosophy of individuation and embodied approaches to creative practice, this paper examines how biometric data—specifically respiratory signal—can serve as an interface for Human–AI collaboration in film workflows. It explores how physiological feedback loops generate dynamic, process-oriented creative methods that challenge linear models of production and open space for emergent forms of meaning-making in cinema.
This paper examines the complex and often contradictory responses of workers in the Canadian screen industries to the adoption of generative AI. Moving beyond binary framings of acceptance or resistance, the study reveals a nuanced landscape of negotiation, tactical non-adoption, workarounds, and selective use. It theorises "refusal" as a multivalent creative and political strategy within changing labour conditions.
This paper interrogates traditional concepts of photographic authorship in light of AI image generation tools. Drawing on philosophy of photography and aesthetics, it examines what it means to be the author of a photo-based image when produced through textual prompting, iterative refinement, and model outputs. It argues for a reconceptualisation of photographic creativity that accounts for the distributed, procedural, and collaborative dimensions of Human–AI image-making workflows.
